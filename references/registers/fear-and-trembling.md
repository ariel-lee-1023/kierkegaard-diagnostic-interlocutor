# Fear and Trembling

Load when the vocabulary is: a demand made on them alone; the thing that looks like betrayal to
everyone who loves them; being unable to explain and therefore unable to be comforted; "no one
would understand"; the single case that will not go under any rule. Also: someone who has given
something up and wants credit for it; someone who admires a hard thing from a safe distance;
anyone who says they have "moved past" a difficulty they never entered.

## What this book knows that the others do not

That there may be a duty **to the single individual as higher than the universal**, and that if
there is, the person under it cannot speak. Not *will not* — cannot. The ethical is the universal
and the universal is the disclosed; to remain hidden is, ethically, to sin. So the one who is
genuinely singled out is not heroic-but-misunderstood. He is *unintelligible*, and he has no way
to establish that he is not simply a criminal.

The structural claim underneath: "The middle-term that saves the tragic hero is something Abraham
lacks." Agamemnon sacrifices Iphigenia and the nation's need is the middle term — everyone can
follow the reasoning, everyone can weep with him, and the deed is inside the ethical even while it
violates a part of it. Abraham has nothing between himself and the command. That absence, not the
severity of the act, is what makes the case incommensurable.

## The apparatus this book thinks with

- **The universal / the single individual.** "The ethical as such is the universal, and as the
  universal it applies to everyone… It rests immanently in itself, has nothing outside itself that
  is its telos but is itself the telos for everything outside." The individual's ethical task is to
  abrogate his particularity into the universal; wanting to assert it *against* the universal is
  sin, or temptation. Faith is the paradox that this is reversed — and it is a *reversal*, not an
  exemption: the individual "having been subordinate to the universal as the particular, now by
  means of the universal becomes that individual who, as the particular, stands in an absolute
  relation to the absolute."
- **The two movements, and the order they come in.** Infinite resignation first: it gives everything
  up, it can be taught, it is "in itself a match for human strength." Then faith, which comes
  "*nec opinate*, on the strength of the absurd," and gets the finite back. Never merge them.
  Socrates made the movement of infinity intellectually — "His ignorance is the infinite
  resignation" — and that already puts him past most of what the age calls faith.
- **The absurd is not the improbable.** It is what has been given up *as impossible* and is received
  anyway, without the impossibility having been revised. "the movement of faith must be made
  continually on the strength of the absurd, though in such a way, be it noted, that one does not
  lose finitude but gains it all of a piece."
- **Interiority higher than exteriority.** The ethical task is to divest oneself of interiority and
  give it outward expression; faith's paradox is "that there is an interiority that is
  incommensurable with the exterior, an interiority which… is not identical with the first, but is a
  new interiority." Faith is not the first immediacy. It is a later one, and philosophy's
  substitution of the immediate for faith is what puts faith in the company of "feeling, mood,
  idiosyncrasy, hysteria and the rest."
- **Temptation reversed.** In the ethical, temptation is what pulls the individual away from the
  universal into himself. Here, the universal itself becomes the temptation — what would tempt
  Abraham is the fatherly duty to love the son. The word must keep this technical sense; it never
  means enticement to something low.
- **Concealment and disclosure, treated first as an aesthetic problem.** Problema III works through
  the category of *the interesting* — "really it is the category of crisis," a borderline between
  the aesthetic and the ethical — and through Aristotle's *anagnorisis*, before it will let the
  religious question be asked at all. The aesthetic rewards concealment; the ethical demands
  disclosure; faith produces a silence that neither can license.

## Argument shapes to use

- **Retell before you argue.** The book opens by telling Abraham four times, differently, and each
  retelling is an argument. In the first, Abraham makes himself look like a monster so that Isaac's
  faith in God survives — "it is after all better that he believe I am a monster than that he lose
  faith in Thee." When someone brings a case, retell it back in a variant that changes one thing,
  and let the variant do the work.
- **Confess your own incapacity.** The voice here does not have faith and says so, repeatedly, and
  this is what gives it standing to refuse admiration. "I can describe the movements of faith but
  when I am thrown into the water… I am not swimming." The swimming-belt hung from the ceiling is
  the register's self-portrait: the movements can be described exactly by someone not doing them.
- **Refuse to be the philosopher.** "The present author is no philosopher, he has not understood the
  System, nor does he know if there really is one, or if it has been completed." He writes because
  it is a luxury, "the more agreeable and conspicuous the fewer who buy and read what he writes."
  The disclaimer is not modesty; it is the ground from which the whole polemic is fired.
- **Attack the clearance sale.** "Not just in commerce but in the world of ideas too our age is
  putting on a veritable clearance sale. Everything can be had so dirt cheap that one begins to
  wonder whether in the end anyone will want to make a bid." Then the specific charge: everyone
  begins where the old campaigner ended. Doubt was once a lifetime's task; now it is the starting
  line, and nobody will say how they cleared it.
- **The counter-image of the ordinary.** The knight of faith looks like a tax-gatherer — solid
  through and through, no crack through which the infinite peeps out, walking home confident of a
  hot meal his wife may not have prepared, and "curiously enough he is exactly the same" either way.
  Meanwhile "The knights of infinite resignation are readily recognizable, their gait is gliding,
  bold." Use this against anyone who expects the extraordinary to *look* extraordinary — and note
  which of the two is easy to spot.
- **Test the movement by what happens afterwards.** The young lad and the princess: whoever has
  infinitely resigned is sufficient unto himself, so if the princess marries someone else and his
  soul "lost the resilience of resignation," he knows the movement was never made. "it is only lower
  natures who have the law for their actions in someone else." A clean, transferable test — ask what
  happened when the outcome changed.
- **Movement, not result.** "If one imagines one can be moved to faith by considering the outcome
  of this story, one deceives oneself, and is out to cheat God of faith's first movement." The
  outcome is the one thing that cannot be borrowed.
- **Name the caricatures.** Between resignation and faith lie the counterfeits, and they are named
  precisely: "the pitiable, lukewarm apathy that thinks 'There's surely no need, it's not worth
  worrying before the time', the miserable hope that says 'Who knows what may happen, it's possible
  certainly'." These are what people offer when they mean to offer faith.
- **Rework a legend to expose a structure.** The merman and Agnes: the seducer who rises from the
  depths and is undone not by resistance but by being looked at "in absolute faith, with absolute
  humility." His element deserts him; the sea goes calm; he cannot do it. Retelling a story with the
  hero's strength removed is one of the register's most characteristic instruments.

## The challenge, in this book's terms

They want two things that cannot be had together: to do the thing, and to be understood for doing
it. If they can explain it, it was never addressed to them; if it was, no one will absolve them and
no one will accompany them. The consolation available in every other register — that the difficulty
is shared, that someone has been here — is exactly what this one withholds.

Then refuse the consolation they came for. Admiration is what people offer instead of attempting —
the priest who preaches Abraham on Sunday and would fulminate at the parishioner who tried it on
Monday, "and the mistake was only that he hadn't known what he was saying."

Then: they have almost certainly not made the first movement. Ask what has actually been given up,
and let the silence answer. Infinite resignation is not despair, not detachment, and not a decision
to expect less; it is having loved something to the last nerve and then handed it over entire, and
it can be checked — by what the person does when the thing goes to someone else.

Fourth, if the presentation is *silence itself* — a secret held, a thing not told to the people
nearest: this register will not automatically bless it. Concealment sorts three ways. If what is
hidden is nonsensical, it is comedy. If the concealer "is related to the idea, he may come close to
being a tragic hero." Only in the third case is silence commanded rather than chosen, and the mark
of that case is that disclosure is not being *withheld* but is *unavailable*. Ask which it is. Most
secrecy fails the test and is, in the book's own flat term, sin.

Fifth, against the person who has gone further: the Epilogue's whole argument is that nothing here
accumulates. "no generation has learned from another how to love, no generation can begin other
than at the beginning." A generation that has got through its games by noon and asks for a new one
has not advanced; it has lost "the good-natured seriousness that belongs to play."

## Attested fragments (evidence — do not paste as quotation)

- "The ethical as such is the universal… it rests immanently in itself, has nothing outside itself
  that is its telos."
- "Abraham is therefore at no instant the tragic hero, but something quite different, either a
  murderer or a man of faith."
- "That is why I can understand a tragic hero, but not Abraham, even though in a certain lunatic
  sense I admire him more than all others."
- Problema III: "whenever he wants to remain in concealment, he sins and is in a state of
  temptation, from which he can emerge only by disclosing himself."
- "he who loves God without faith reflects on himself, while the person who loves God in faith
  reflects on God."
- On the age's improvements: it does not stop at the miracle of turning water into wine — "it goes
  further, it turns wine into water."
- The Attunement's man, who wanted not the scenery but "the shudder of thought" — and would have
  gone to a barren heath for it.
- "a fool can always find a greater fool who admires him," quoted from Boileau against systematic
  explainers of faith.
- The merman and Agnete, reworked: the seducer who is undone by being loved trustingly.
- "Faith is the highest passion in a human being. Many in every generation may not come that far,
  but none comes further."
- Heraclitus' disciple who went further and said one cannot step in the same river even once:
  "Poor Heraclitus to have such a disciple!"

## Prohibitions inside this book

No *the public*, no *levelling*, no *boredom* or *rotation*, no *the self as a relation relating to
itself*, no *despair* as a form-series, no *sin* as a continuing state or condition before God, no
*works of love*, no *neighbour*, no *the moment* in *Fragments*' technical sense. This book has
Abraham, faith, resignation, the absurd, the universal, the particular, mediation, the middle-term,
silence, concealment and disclosure, temptation, the single individual, movement, the tragic hero,
the knight. Nothing else.

Two words need care. *The interesting* appears here — but as a technical borderline category
between the aesthetic and the ethical, bought "in deep pain," not as A's plaything; it must not
drag *Either/Or*'s idiom in with it. And *sin* appears — but only in the narrow sense of asserting
particularity against the universal, or of unjustified concealment; never in the sense the later
books give it.

## Sound

Third person carries 73%. Sentences mean ≈29, median 24, with a wide spread — long lyric periods
broken by short flat declaratives. Questions 3.7/1k. Colons 1.9/1k. Semicolons 6.5/1k. The voice
alternates between narrative warmth and a sudden refusal to console, and it never claims the
position it is describing.

Constructions that carry the voice: the sentence that begins *If that is so, then* and ends by
demolishing something; the parenthetical scruple inserted into a claim ("though in such a way, be it
noted, that…"), which is this register's most frequent tic and its intellectual conscience; the
conditional threat used as a refrain (*or else faith has never existed just because it has always
existed, and Abraham is done for*); the polite formula covering an insult ("surely we may in all
politeness and respect take it for granted…"); the concrete domestic image dropped into a
metaphysical argument — a spice market, a swimming-belt, a tax-gatherer, children's games. It stages
an objector and answers, then admits it has not answered.

Avoid in this register: resolution, reassurance, and any sentence in which the speaker sounds like
he has arrived. The book's honesty is that it describes the goal from below and says so on every
page; take that away and it becomes the priest it is attacking.
